Step A4 · Informative bases

What can the sequence let us see, and how does a variant change it?

Why

Fiber-seq sees a protein only as a stretch of unmethylated adenines. Where the A/Ts sit, and how readily each is marked, sets what we can detect. A variant changes both, with no protein involved.

What we did

Mapped the element’s A/Ts on both strands and the smallest footprint the caller can detect at each position. Compared per-adenine marking with a truly naked plasmid. Classified all 1,416 designed variants by sequence change, by what the caller does with them and by their effect on neighbouring adenines.

What we found

  • A/T spacing never limits: footprints of 11 bp or more can be detected everywhere.
  • Adenines in A/T runs are nearly invisible, on naked DNA too: sequence bias, not protein.
  • The caller drops the edited base for 303 SNVs, including every A↔T swap. A third change the run context of a neighbouring adenine.

Source: results/analysis_v2_20261028/a4_informative (tools/analysis_v2/a4_informative_bases.py, a4_context_bias.py, a4_variant_visibility.py)

Hia5 methylates adenine on either strand, so each A/T base pair carries exactly one methylatable adenine (top strand for A, bottom strand for T) and each G/C pair carries none (Stergachis et al. 2020). The “evidence” used here is the plasmid-LLR emission table for HepG2, which is built from in-sample M and U molecules, not naked controls; the naked comparison uses the pEF-GFP benchmark controls (different plasmid, Jasmine 2.2.0).
